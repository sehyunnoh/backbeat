Notes on a normal staff tell you a pitch. A drum has no pitch to write, so drum music uses the staff as a **map**: each line or space stands for one drum or cymbal. How high a note sits tells you **which** instrument to hit.

## The parts of a drum staff

- **Five lines.** Count them from the bottom: line 1 is the lowest, line 5 is the top.
- **The percussion clef:** two short vertical bars at the start. It tells you this is drum music, not pitched notes.
- **The time signature:** two numbers stacked at the start. **4 over 4** means four quarter-note beats in each bar.
- **Bar lines:** vertical lines that split the music into bars.
- **Count:** the numbers and "&" under the staff on this site help you line notes up with beats.

## Which drum is where

<NotationKey />

Two simple rules help you remember:

1. **Lower drums sit lower.** The kick is at the bottom, the snare is in the middle, and the high tom is near the top.
2. **Cymbals use an ×.** Hi-hat, ride and crash are ×. Drums (snare, toms, kick) are round, filled notes.

## Hands up, feet down

In most drum music, the **stems** show which limbs play:

- Stems pointing **up** are for the **hands** (hi-hat, snare, toms, cymbals).
- Stems pointing **down** are for the **feet** (kick).

That keeps the two voices easy to see apart. In the example below, the hi-hat and snare notes have stems going up, and the kick has stems going down.

## Extra marks

| Mark | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ○ above an × | Open hi-hat |
| > above or below a note | Accent: play it louder |
| ( ) around a note | Ghost note: play it very quietly |
